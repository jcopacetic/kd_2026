A content strategy is a plan for what you publish, who it's for, and what it should get them to do next. A good one starts from a business goal and your buyers, organizes topics into clusters you can become known for, turns each strong piece into several formats, and measures whether any of it moves people toward a sale. AI search has changed how people find content, but it hasn't changed that: buyers still need help at each stage of their decision, and the companies that give it get remembered.

This guide covers where content strategy came from, what AI search changed, and how I'd build one today.

## A short history

Content strategy is older than the internet. It used to be called branded content.

- **1895:** John Deere started publishing *The Furrow*, a magazine that helped farmers run more profitable farms. It sold no tractors directly and became one of the best-known early examples of value-first content.
- **1900:** Michelin launched the *Michelin Guide*. It encouraged people to drive more, and people who drive more buy more tires.
- **Mid-1900s:** Brands embedded themselves in mass media. "Soap operas" got their name from the soap companies, Procter & Gamble among them, that sponsored them. Advertorials blurred the line between editorial and advertising. Content was expensive and the media channels controlled access, so the strategy was to own a moment and wrap the brand in a story.
- **The internet:** Publishing got cheap. Email newsletters, blogs and websites let brands talk to customers directly, and search engines made structure and keywords matter. Companies like Red Bull and HubSpot began operating like media companies. The idea that took hold: if you answer your customers' questions, you become their trusted advisor before the sale.
- **2009–2010s:** Content strategy became a discipline of its own. Kristina Halvorson's *Content Strategy for the Web* (2009) is still a standard reference. In B2B, HubSpot's inbound methodology made content the main source of leads. In B2C, YouTube and Instagram brought user-generated content and influencer marketing.
- **2020s:** Generative AI made producing content almost free, which made the strategy and the trust behind it worth more.

The progression is from "let's write a brochure" to "let's build a system that gets the right content to the right person at the right time, in a format they'll use, to help them take the next step." When producing content gets easier, getting attention gets harder. John Deere and Michelin stood out because few others were doing it. Today everyone is.

## What AI search changed

For most of the web's history, the goal was the first page of Google, because almost nobody looked past it. Paid search changed that early on by letting businesses buy their way above the organic results. Featured snippets then started answering questions directly on the results page. Now AI does it at scale.

Google's AI Overviews and AI Mode, ChatGPT, Perplexity and the rest answer the question themselves and cite a few sources. The effect on clicks is measurable:

- When an AI Overview appears, the top organic result for an informational search gets about 58% fewer clicks than it would without one ([Ahrefs](https://ahrefs.com/blog/ai-overviews-reduce-clicks-update/)).
- In a Pew study of real browsing, people clicked a result 8% of the time when an AI summary appeared and 15% of the time when it didn't ([Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/22/google-users-are-less-likely-to-click-on-links-when-an-ai-summary-appears-in-the-results/)).
- AI Overviews show up far more on informational searches than on commercial ones: 36% against 8% in one large study ([Seer Interactive](https://www.seerinteractive.com/insights/aio-impact-on-google-ctr-2026-update)).
- Being cited inside the AI Overview roughly doubles the clicks you get compared with being left out of it (same Seer study).

What that means for your content:

1. **Simple how-to content loses the most.** If an AI can answer the question in two sentences, it will, and the click goes nowhere. Write what an AI can't easily summarize: real examples, your own data, working code, specific mistakes and how to fix them, opinions backed by experience.
2. **Answer first, then go deep.** Put a direct two- or three-sentence answer under each question-shaped heading. That's what gets quoted. The depth underneath is what earns the visit.
3. **Be the source of something.** Original numbers, a named framework, a template people reuse. Others cite sources, and AI tools pick up what gets cited.
4. **Get mentioned off your own site.** Answers in communities, guest posts, podcasts and reviews put your name in more places AI tools read.
5. **Measure differently.** Clicks alone will undercount you. Track impressions in Search Console, visits from AI tools in your analytics, and ask new leads how they found you.

## Is a content strategy still worth it?

Yes. The way people find content keeps changing, but you still have to say something to be heard, and buyers still do most of their research before they talk to anyone. Nobody has mastered AI search yet. You don't need to. You need a clear goal, a clear audience, and the discipline to publish useful things consistently.

## What a content strategy is

It's a plan for publishing over time, built around a goal. The content doesn't have to sell directly. Michelin never published a tire catalog and told people to buy. It made them want to take road trips, and it was there when their tires wore out.

Two inputs shape everything else:

- **Buyer personas:** who you're trying to reach, what they care about, and where they get information.
- **The buyer's journey:** the stages they go through, from noticing a problem to choosing a vendor to getting value after the sale.

Each piece of content should speak to one persona at one stage and point to the next step. I cover how personas, journey stages and campaigns fit together in [Aligning Buyer Personas, Journeys, Campaigns & Pipelines](/insights/aligning-buyer-personas-journeys-campaigns-pipelines/).

## How to build one

### 1. Set the goal

Pick one primary goal for the next six to twelve months: more qualified leads, shorter sales cycles, better retention, or authority in a specific niche. Everything you plan should serve it.

### 2. Find your topics

Start with the questions your customers already ask. Sales calls, support tickets and onboarding conversations are the best source, because they're in your buyers' own words.

Then check demand:

- **Google Search Console** shows the searches you already appear for, including the ones where you rank on page two or three and could move up.
- **A keyword tool** (Ahrefs, Semrush or similar) shows search volume and how hard each term is to rank for.
- **AI assistants** are useful for brainstorming related questions and grouping them. Check every suggestion against Search Console or a keyword tool before you plan around it.

### 3. Organize topics into clusters

Choose a few core topics you want to be known for. Each gets a **pillar page** that covers it broadly, supported by a **cluster** of narrower articles that each answer one specific question and link back to the pillar. This structure helps readers move through the material and helps search engines understand what you're an authority on.

I map clusters in a mind-mapping tool. I use **Whimsical**, but any tool that draws a tree works. Seeing the topics and their connections makes gaps obvious.

### 4. Decide your formats and process

Answer these before you create anything:

- **Where do ideas come from?** Customer questions, sales calls, industry changes, competitor gaps. Pick a consistent source.
- **What's the first format?** An article, a video or a podcast episode. This becomes the master version everything else is cut from.
- **What will you turn it into?** A video summary, a short email, social posts, an infographic, a slide in a sales deck.
- **What's the order and schedule?** A content calendar with realistic time estimates per format.
- **Where does each format go?** Website, email, LinkedIn, YouTube, communities. Not every format works on every channel.
- **How will you link it together?** Every piece links to its pillar and to two or three related pieces.

### 5. Create

Write or record the master piece yourself, or with someone who knows the subject. AI tools are good at proofreading, tightening, checking tone and gathering references to verify. They also now generate images, slides and video, which makes repurposing much cheaper than it was. They aren't a substitute for experience. Content that only restates what's already online gives AI search no reason to cite you and readers no reason to remember you.

### 6. Repurpose

One strong piece can feed an entire content ecosystem. Some people want the long article. Some will watch a five-minute video of the same material. Some will only stop for an infographic or a short post. Cutting the master piece into those formats reaches all of them for far less work than starting over each time.

Focus repurposing on your best performers, and on the formats your personas use. A TikTok series is wasted effort if your buyers are CFOs who read email.

You don't need a big budget. Contractors for infographics or video editing can be hired by the piece, and AI tools now cover much of the simpler design and editing. HubSpot is a good model here. It's a very large company, yet its training videos are simple: a presenter, a microphone and slides. The material is good and the production is plain, and that's enough.

### 7. Distribute and connect

Search is one channel among several. Email, social media, webinars, communities, partners and word of mouth all carry content. Write each piece so it hints at the next step without pushing a "buy now" before the reader is close to deciding.

### 8. Measure and adjust

Track, at minimum:

- Search Console impressions and average position for each cluster
- Visits from search and from AI tools
- Engagement: time on page, clicks to related content and to your offer
- Leads and opportunities that touched content, from your CRM
- Which pieces get cited or linked by others

Review it monthly. Update pieces that are slipping with real changes, not new dates, and put more effort into the clusters that bring in buyers.

## The principles that haven't changed

People don't want to be sold to; they want help. Write for your ideal customer first; search engines and AI tools reward the same thing. Organize your knowledge into pillars and clusters, reuse your best work across formats, and measure what moves people toward a decision. The tools will keep changing. Being useful to the right person at the right moment keeps working.
