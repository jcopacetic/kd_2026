A buyer-centric campaign is built for one kind of buyer at one stage of their decision. Start with who they are and what they need right now, write the message and choose the formats for that person and that moment, then track every asset so you can see what works and adjust. Campaigns built the other way round, starting from a product or a channel, end up talking to nobody in particular.

This guide covers the two inputs every campaign needs, how to build one step by step, and how to track it. I use a running example throughout: Bob, the CEO.

## The two inputs: personas and the journey

### Buyer personas

A buyer persona is a profile of an ideal customer, built from real data: customer interviews, CRM records, sales calls and support tickets. Many campaigns skip this or work from assumptions. You may know your audience instinctively, but your team, your contractors and your future self need it written down.

Start by asking who you most want as a customer:

- Who gets the most value from what you sell?
- Who is easiest to work with?
- Who is most likely to buy, and later to recommend you?

Then build a profile for each: where they work, their role, their main pain points, where they look for help, the questions they ask, and how they like to be communicated with. Give each one a name and a face, and make the profiles easy to find for everyone working on your marketing, in-house or contracted.

Personas change what a campaign says and how it sounds. A 10%-off coupon needs little messaging and appeals to almost anyone. An offer to advise startup CEOs on their online presence won't land with a marketing manager at a large company. Tone matters just as much. "Dude" and "crazy cool" might sell candy bars; they won't sell professional services.

I go deeper on persona research, and what to capture for each persona, in [Aligning Buyer Personas, Journeys, Campaigns & Pipelines](/insights/aligning-buyer-personas-journeys-campaigns-pipelines/).

### The buyer's journey

The idea behind the buyer's journey is old. The AIDA model (attention, interest, desire, action) dates to the late 1890s, and the purchase funnel and B2B buying-cycle models followed in the 20th century. You may know it as "the funnel": broad messages at the top, more targeted ones in the middle, and a sale at the bottom.

HubSpot's inbound methodology popularized the buyer's journey in the 2010s and added a stage after the sale, Delight, so customers don't simply drop out of the bottom of the funnel. I still use a linear version of that journey in my consulting because sales, marketing, operations and support pipelines can all be mapped onto it:

1. **Awareness:** the buyer has noticed a problem or question but can't define it well yet. They aren't ready to talk about solutions.
2. **Consideration:** they understand the problem and are researching ways to solve it. They aren't ready to buy.
3. **Decision:** they're comparing specific vendors or products.
4. **Delight:** they've bought, and the goal now is success, then advocacy.

The stages can be adapted. For a live streamer, an "audience journey" fits better: **unaware**, **aware and curious**, **new viewer**, **loyal viewer**, **donor**, **subscriber**. There are several conversion points (new viewer, donor, subscriber), and the "customer" is looking for entertainment rather than solving a problem. The difference from traditional marketing is that the content is the message, and the whole audience sees all of it.

### Where the flywheel fits

At INBOUND 2018, HubSpot replaced the funnel in its own methodology with the **flywheel**: three stages, Attract, Engage and Delight, arranged in a circle around growth. The idea is that happy customers create momentum by bringing in new ones, which puts customer success at the center of growth.

The flywheel and the linear journey don't conflict. Awareness, consideration and decision still happen inside Attract and Engage. I find the linear version easier for planning campaigns and pipelines, because each stage has a clear owner and a clear next step, while the flywheel is a better way to explain why Delight matters.

## What a marketing campaign is

A campaign is an organized effort toward one marketing goal. You define the goal, build the assets that serve it, and plan how to get them in front of the right people.

What separates a professional campaign from a burst of activity is measurement. Treat every campaign as a hypothesis: launch it, track the results, keep what works and change what doesn't.

Campaigns vary in length and scope. Some run continuously and evolve; others are monthly, seasonal or one-off. Some cover every journey stage for every persona; others target one persona at one stage.

## How to build one

### 1. Pick the persona and the stage

A great campaign starts where one persona meets one journey stage.

Take **Bob**, a CEO in the awareness stage. He's starting to realize his sales team needs automation, and he doesn't want to add headcount to get it. Bob isn't ready for a pitch, and selling to him now would likely scare him off. What he needs is help defining the problem.

### 2. Set the goal

Write down what the campaign should achieve and how you'll measure it. For Bob's awareness campaign, the goal might be getting CEOs at companies like his to download a short guide on modernizing sales processes, measured by downloads from the right job titles and the share who later engage with consideration-stage content.

### 3. Write the message for that person

Bob's content should explain the cost of outdated sales practices and what upgrading looks like, without selling a product. Helping him define his problem makes you the expert he remembers when he's ready to look at solutions.

Get the register right. Bob is a senior decision-maker focused on the big picture, so skip the technical jargon, and don't swing so casual that it undercuts his position. He spends his days in meetings and his inbox. Make the content easy, warm and quick to get through, and give him a clear next step.

### 4. Choose formats your persona actually uses

For Bob, that might be a short, friendly video he can play while working through his inbox, or a clear infographic he can take in at a glance. A 4,000-word white paper probably isn't it.

Pick formats by persona:

- If your buyers don't watch videos, an expensive video won't pay off.
- If they find infographics lightweight, a beautifully designed one is wasted.
- If they won't read long posts, a campaign built only on long posts won't connect.

Valuable information eventually wins in any format, but matching format to persona gets you there faster.

### 5. Build the supporting assets

Around the core content, a campaign usually needs some combination of:

- **Landing pages** for the offer
- **Forms** that ask only for what you need at this stage
- **Calls to action** in every piece, pointing to the next step
- **Emails** to deliver the content and follow up
- **Ads** to reach the persona beyond your existing audience
- **Social posts** on the platforms the persona uses
- **Events or webinars** for later-stage buyers

Keep the design, messaging and goal consistent across all of them. The buyer should feel they're moving through one experience.

### 6. Decide which stages the campaign covers

- **A full-funnel campaign** covers every stage, from awareness through decision and beyond. It suits long-term nurturing: educational content for awareness, case studies in consideration, a tailored offer at decision.
- **A targeted campaign** covers one stage, such as a decision-stage offer for buyers who are already comparing vendors. It suits fixing a specific weak point in your pipeline.

Bob's campaign starts as a targeted awareness campaign. If it works, the natural next step is a consideration campaign for the CEOs it brought in.

## Track everything

Measure each campaign as a whole and each asset in it. My rule of thumb: monitor the hypothesis, repeat what succeeds, adjust what fails.

Conversions alone aren't enough. You also need to know how many people saw the campaign, where they dropped off, and which assets moved them forward. That's what lets you make small adjustments while it runs and plan better campaigns afterwards.

The tools:

- **HubSpot** Marketing Hub Professional and Enterprise include a campaigns tool that groups emails, pages, ads, social posts and CTAs and reports on them together, including the contacts and deals they influenced.
- **Google Analytics** can do much of the same with consistent UTM parameters on every link and well-planned conversion events. It takes more setup and won't connect to deals in your CRM on its own.

Whichever you use, agree on a UTM naming convention before launch. Campaign data is only as good as the tagging.

## Final notes

Once you understand your ideal customer and the journey they take from not knowing you exist to recommending you, campaigns get much easier. Map one persona to one stage and the right message is often obvious.

If you need sharper personas, consider bringing in a persona specialist. A budget project works from your existing knowledge and analytics. A thorough one includes interviews with stakeholders, other departments, and current or past customers. One caution: never pull a prospect into a persona interview. It comes across as a sales tactic, and it can cost you the deal.

Track everything, in as much detail as you can manage. Good marketing is closer to behavioral science than to guesswork: understand who benefits from what you sell, where they spend their time and how they take in information, then meet them there.
