This framework connects four layers that most companies run separately. **Buyer personas** describe who you sell to. The **buyer's journey** describes how they decide, in four stages from Awareness to Delight. **Campaigns** are built for each persona at each stage. **Pipelines** in the CRM then move each lead, deal and customer through six connected processes: Leads, Sales, Onboarding, Operations, Delight and Support.

The point is the connections. Marketing, sales and customer success each optimize their own numbers, and the customer feels every gap between them: leads that sales can't use, deals closed without a plan for onboarding, support cut off from everything that came before. When the four layers share one structure, every handoff carries its context forward.

## Why it's worth the effort

Retention is where the economics show up. Research by Frederick Reichheld of Bain & Company found that increasing customer retention rates by 5% increases profits by 25% to 95% ([HBR](https://hbr.org/2014/10/the-value-of-keeping-the-right-customers)). Retention depends on everything before it: who you attracted, what they were promised, and how well the handoff after the sale went.

A simple test: can your team describe its buyers, their journey, the campaigns built for each stage, and the pipeline that governs each handoff? If not, you're running tactics without a system.

## 1. Buyer personas: who you serve

A buyer persona is a semi-fictional profile of an ideal customer, built from customer interviews, CRM data and sales conversations. Demographics alone don't predict buying behavior. A useful persona captures goals, fears, decision criteria, information sources and objections.

A persona makes a market segment concrete enough to act on. "SMB decision-makers" is hard to write for. "Operations Owen", a VP of Operations at a 50-person manufacturer who cares about uptime and dislikes vendor lock-in, gives the team something specific for copy, channel choice and workflow design.

### What to capture, and where to find it

| Dimension | What to capture | Where to find it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Role and responsibilities | Job title, team size, scope of authority, daily workflow | LinkedIn, job postings, customer interviews |
| Goals and KPIs | What they're measured on; what success looks like to them | Win/loss interviews, sales call recordings |
| Pain points and fears | Operational frustrations, risks they're avoiding, past failures | Customer interviews, support tickets, reviews |
| Buying triggers | Events that prompt them to look for a solution | CRM opportunity data, marketing attribution |
| Information sources | Blogs, analysts, peers, events, trade publications | Website analytics, source attribution |
| Objections | Common reasons for not buying, or for delaying | Sales call recordings, lost-deal analysis |
| Decision criteria | How they evaluate vendors; what gets a deal approved | Proposal feedback, procurement conversations |

### Plan for the buying group

Most B2B purchases involve several people with different needs. A software sale might include a champion (the operational user pushing for it), an economic buyer (the CFO approving budget), a technical evaluator (IT or security), and end users whose adoption decides whether the purchase succeeds. Map messaging, content and objection handling to every role in the group, including the ones you rarely talk to.

Start with two or three well-researched personas. Six shallow ones help less than two deep ones, and each should rest on real customer interviews checked against what your CRM data shows.

## 2. The buyer's journey: four stages, including after the sale

The classic funnel of Awareness, Consideration and Decision stops at the sale. Customers who get no structure after they buy are the ones who churn quietly or stop recommending you. This framework adds a fourth stage, **Delight**, covering onboarding, ongoing success and advocacy.

| Stage | Buyer's mindset | What they need | Marketing goal | Sales role |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Awareness | Knows the problem, not the solution | Education and validation | Reach and capture | None, or SDR outreach |
| Consideration | Comparing approaches and categories | Proof and comparison | Nurture and differentiate | Consultative conversations |
| Decision | Comparing specific vendors | Confidence and lower risk | Enable sales with proof | Active deal management |
| Delight | A customer, looking for the promised value | Onboarding and ongoing success | Retention and advocacy | Account management and expansion |

**Awareness.** The buyer is researching their situation and isn't ready to talk to vendors. Conversions here are low-commitment: an email signup, a download, an event registration.

**Consideration.** The problem is defined, and the buyer is comparing approaches, including building it themselves. They look for case studies, comparisons and expert opinion. Sales can join as an advisor.

**Decision.** The shortlist is set, criteria firm up, and economic buyers join. Demos, proposals, references, trials and ROI estimates do the work. Marketing's job is to equip sales with battlecards, calculators and reference lists.

**Delight.** The contract is signed and retention starts. Treat it as a stage with its own campaigns, pipelines and metrics: structured onboarding, proactive check-ins, value milestones, and a deliberate path to referrals and expansion.

## 3. Campaigns: one per persona per stage

A campaign is a coordinated set of activities aimed at one audience, toward one goal, over a set period. In this framework, campaigns are defined where a persona meets a journey stage, rather than per channel or per product. Three personas and four stages give twelve campaign tracks, each with its own messaging, assets, calls to action, channels and success metrics.

### The assets a campaign draws on

| Asset | Best stages | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Blog posts | Awareness | Educational, found through search; written in the persona's vocabulary |
| Landing pages | All | One per persona and stage, with proof and a call to action that fits their readiness |
| Downloads (ebooks, infographics, case studies) | Consideration, Decision | Worth exchanging contact details for; case studies carry the most weight late in the journey |
| Events and webinars | Consideration, Decision | Live conversation builds trust faster |
| Calls to action | All | Every piece should point to the next logical step |
| Forms | All | Ask only for what's needed now and build the profile over time |
| Emails | All | Persona-specific sequences triggered by behavior |
| Social media | Awareness, retargeting | Pick platforms by persona: LinkedIn for B2B decision-makers, YouTube for complex products |
| Ads | Awareness, retargeting | Most effective when aimed at a persona audience with stage-appropriate creative |
| Analytics | All | UTM discipline, CRM integration and revenue attribution tie engagement to pipeline movement |

### What persona-specific messaging looks like

It means changing the value proposition, the kind of proof and the language for each buyer. Swapping the first name in an email doesn't count. Here's one product described for three personas:

| Feature | Ops leader | CFO | IT director |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Automated reporting | Ends three hours a week of manual data pulls, so the team works on decisions instead of spreadsheets. | Cuts reporting labor costs by about $18K a year and improves data accuracy for audits. | API-based reporting with role-based access, audit logs and SOC 2-compliant data handling. |
| Onboarding support | A dedicated specialist learns your workflow and configures the system to fit your team. | Full implementation included at no extra cost, with a 90-day time-to-value guarantee. | Technical onboarding covering SSO, IP allowlisting, custom webhooks and full API documentation. |

*Sample copy for a fictional product.*

## 4. The funnel and the pipelines are different tools

People mix these up constantly. The **marketing funnel** describes how groups of buyers move through awareness, consideration and decision, measured in volumes and conversion rates. **Pipelines** live in the CRM and track individual records (a lead, a deal, a ticket) through defined stages with owners and tasks.

| | Marketing funnel | Operational pipeline |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unit | A population or cohort | An individual record (deal, contact, company) |
| Main users | Marketing leadership | Sales reps, customer success, support |
| Measures | Volume, conversion rate, velocity, cost per stage | Stage, owner, expected close date, activity |
| Output | Strategy, budget allocation, targeting | Day-to-day work, forecasts, tasks |
| Time horizon | Monthly or quarterly trends | Real time |
| In the CRM | Reports, dashboards, attribution models | Deal and ticket pipelines with stage-based workflows |

You need both. A team that only manages the funnel loses track of individual buyers. A team that only manages pipelines can't see where to put campaign budget. Campaigns move the funnel, and pipelines move each record.

## 5. Six pipelines across the customer lifecycle

Most companies run a leads process and a sales pipeline. This framework runs six, so every phase of the relationship has an owner, stages and metrics.

### Leads

Qualifies and routes inquiries before they reach sales, so reps spend time on real opportunities. It's fed by Awareness and Consideration conversions. Leads are scored, enriched and routed by persona fit, intent and ideal-customer match.

- **New lead:** not yet qualified; enrichment running
- **MQL:** met the engagement threshold; passed to an SDR
- **SAL:** SDR confirmed basic fit; passed to an account executive
- **SQL:** AE confirmed budget, authority, need and timing; moved to Sales
- **Disqualified:** returned to nurture or archived

### Sales

Manages deals from the first sales conversation to won or lost. Five to seven stages is usually right, each with clear entry and exit criteria. Name stages after what the buyer has done ("Discovery complete", "Proposal sent") rather than what the seller did ("Contacted", "Following up"), because buyer actions are better signs of progress.

- **Discovery:** needs confirmed
- **Solution design:** scope or proposal defined
- **Proposal sent:** formal proposal or quote delivered
- **Negotiation:** commercial terms under discussion
- **Closed won:** contract signed; starts the Onboarding pipeline
- **Closed lost:** reason recorded; contact returned to nurture

### Onboarding

Gets new customers to their first real result as quickly as possible. Early success is the strongest predictor of whether a customer stays, so this is the most important stage after the sale. It starts automatically at Closed won, has a named owner (a customer success manager or onboarding specialist), and ends when an adoption milestone is reached, rather than after a fixed number of days.

- **Kickoff scheduled:** welcome sent; kickoff call booked
- **Kickoff complete:** success criteria, timeline and stakeholders agreed
- **Configuration in progress:** setup, integrations, data migration
- **Training complete:** key users trained; adoption baseline set
- **First value achieved:** the agreed first milestone is met
- **Onboarding complete:** handed to Operations

### Operations (the cycle)

Runs the relationship during the contract: regular touchpoints, health monitoring, renewals and expansion. It repeats, so a customer may go around it once per renewal period or project phase. Its job is to cover the quiet months between onboarding and the next renewal, when customers most often drift.

- **Active:** steady state; health monitored automatically
- **Health check scheduled:** periodic review, triggered by date or health score
- **Expansion opportunity:** usage or conversations suggest room to grow
- **Renewal in progress:** renewal flagged; commercial review started
- **Renewed:** the cycle starts again

### Delight

Turns satisfied customers into advocates: references, case studies, reviews, referrals and community. It takes deliberate work. Customers enter on a high health score, strong NPS or explicit praise.

- **Advocate identified:** high NPS and engagement
- **Reference requested:** invited to be a reference or case study
- **Referral program joined:** referrals now tracked
- **Review published:** on G2, Capterra, Google or an industry site
- **Active advocate:** joins community events, speaking or co-marketing

### Support

Resolves problems quickly and captures what they reveal about the product and process. It works in both directions with Operations: tickets feed health scores, and proactive health checks prevent tickets. Escalations should alert the account team and, where it matters, sales.

- **Submitted:** logged by email, chat, phone or portal
- **Triage:** category, severity and owner assigned
- **In progress:** being worked on
- **Waiting on customer:** needs a reply or action from them
- **Resolved:** confirmed fixed; satisfaction survey sent
- **Escalated:** high severity; leadership and the account team notified

### Where these live in HubSpot

In HubSpot, this usually means the Lead object (or lead status) for Leads, deal pipelines for Sales, ticket pipelines for Support, and ticket or custom-object pipelines for Onboarding, Operations and Delight. Which object you choose depends on your subscription and on whether the stage needs its own records, reports and automation.

## 6. Handoffs connect the pipelines

The pipelines only pay off when the handoffs between them work. A handoff transfers ownership, context and commitment from one team to the next. When data goes missing or nobody knows who owns the record, the customer notices first.

Every handoff needs three things: a **trigger** (the event that starts it), a **context package** (what travels with the record), and an **acknowledgment** (how the receiving team confirms they've taken it on).

| Handoff | From | To | Trigger | Context that moves with it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| MQL to SDR | Marketing (Leads pipeline) | Sales development (Leads pipeline) | Lead score threshold reached | Persona, source, content engaged, behavior history |
| SQL to AE | SDR | Account executive (Sales pipeline) | Qualification criteria confirmed | Budget, authority, need and timing; discovery notes; objections; persona fit |
| Won to CS | Sales | Customer success (Onboarding pipeline) | Contract signed | Success criteria, key contacts, deal notes, timeline expectations |
| Onboarding to Ops | Onboarding / CS | Account management (Operations pipeline) | First value milestone met | Adoption metrics, stakeholder map, expansion signals |
| Ops to Support | Account management | Support team (Support pipeline) | Customer opens a ticket | Account health, recent activity, ticket history |
| Support to Ops | Support team | Account management | Ticket resolved or escalated | Issue category, resolution, sentiment, risk flag |

### The referral and review loop

The diagram shows a loop from Operations and Delight back into the Sales pipeline. Each advocate produces referrals that arrive without paid acquisition cost, reviews that help convert buyers who are comparing vendors, and proof that shortens objection handling in the Decision stage. Investing in Delight makes acquisition cheaper, in addition to improving retention.

## 7. Rolling it out

**Months 1–2: Foundation**
- Interview 15 to 20 customers and prospects across your target segments.
- Document two or three primary personas using the dimensions above.
- Map the journey for each persona: information sources, decision criteria and objections at each stage.
- Audit your CRM pipelines against the framework for missing stages, unclear entry and exit criteria, and gaps in ownership.
- Record baseline volume, velocity and conversion for each pipeline.

**Months 3–4: Campaigns**
- Build the persona-by-stage matrix, then prioritize the tracks where your pipeline is weakest. If Consideration converts poorly, start there.
- Create the core assets for the priority tracks.
- Set up a UTM convention and attribution for every asset.
- Route leads into persona-specific nurture tracks with CRM workflows.

**Months 5–6: Pipelines**
- Rename stages around buyer actions.
- Write down each handoff's trigger, context package and acknowledgment, then automate the context transfer.
- Launch the Delight pipeline with your top quarter of accounts.
- Connect Support escalations to Operations health scores.

**Ongoing: measure and adjust**
- Keep one revenue dashboard covering funnel and pipeline metrics.
- Refresh persona interviews each quarter.
- Test campaign variations within each persona and stage.
- Review stage conversion monthly and dig into stages that move slowly.
- Track advocacy output: referrals, reviews and reference calls.

## 8. What to measure at each layer

| Layer | Main KPIs | Leading indicators |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Buyer personas | Share of pipeline matching your ideal customer, win rate by persona, contract value by persona | Share of CRM records with a persona assigned, data completeness |
| Buyer's journey | Stage conversion rates, time in stage by persona, drop-off points | Content engagement by stage, CTA click-through |
| Campaigns | MQL volume, cost per lead by track, MQL-to-SQL conversion, campaign-influenced revenue | Email opens and clicks, downloads, webinar attendance |
| Leads pipeline | Lead-to-MQL and MQL-to-SQL rates, response time, disqualification reasons | Lead volume by source, lead score distribution |
| Sales pipeline | Win rate, contract value, sales cycle length, pipeline coverage | Stage conversion, activity per deal, forecast accuracy |
| Onboarding pipeline | Time to first value, onboarding NPS, completion rate, adoption at 30/60/90 days | Time to schedule kickoff, training completion |
| Operations / cycle | Renewal rate, expansion revenue, health score distribution, churn | Health score trend, engagement frequency, product usage |
| Delight pipeline | Referrals, reviews published, reference accounts, advocate NPS | Advocates identified, referral program enrollment |
| Support pipeline | Satisfaction score, resolution time, first-contact resolution, escalation rate | Ticket volume by category, repeat issues |

## Putting it together

None of the four layers does much alone. Good personas don't produce revenue by themselves, and a clean sales pipeline doesn't create advocates. The return comes from the connections: campaigns written for a specific person at a specific moment, handoffs that carry full context, and customers deliberately turned into a source of the next deals.

If you want help mapping this onto your own CRM, the pipelines and handoffs are where I'd start, because that's where most of the gaps show up.
