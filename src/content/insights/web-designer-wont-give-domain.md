If your web designer won't hand over your domain, the first question is whose name is on the registration. If you are the **registrant** (the "registered name holder"), ICANN's rules say you can move the domain: the registrar must give you the transfer code within five calendar days of your request, and can refuse a transfer only for a short list of reasons. If the designer registered it in their own name, you have to get the registration changed to you first, by agreement, through the registrar, or through a lawyer.

Most of these situations end with a polite, specific written request. The rest of this post is the order I'd work in. If the designer has vanished or the agency has closed, start with my [website rescue page for closed agencies](/amarillo/agency-closed/).

## Registrant or account holder: which one are you?

The registrant is the legal holder of the domain. The account holder is whoever logs into the registrar account where the domain sits. They are often different people, and that difference decides how hard this will be.

| Situation | What it means | How hard |
| --- | --- | --- |
| You are the registrant, and the domain is in your own account | You already control it | Easy: log in |
| You are the registrant, and the domain is in the designer's account | You hold the rights, but they hold the login | Usually fixable: ask the registrar directly |
| The designer (or their company) is the registrant | Legally, the registration is in their name | Harder: they have to agree, or you need documents or a lawyer |
| A reseller or "website package" vendor holds it | Depends on their contract and who is listed as registrant | Read the contract, then ask |

Under the current Transfer Policy, the registered name holder is "the only party that has the authority to approve or deny a transfer request." Older setups also listed an "administrative contact", often the designer; under the earlier wording, the registrant's authority already won in a dispute.

To see where the domain sits, search it at [ICANN Lookup](https://lookup.icann.org/). The "Registrar" field tells you which company holds the registration. The registrant's name is often hidden for privacy, so you may need to ask the registrar, or look at old invoices and renewal emails. My [10-minute website ownership check](/insights/who-owns-your-website-ownership-check/) covers the domain and everything else worth checking.

## What does ICANN's Transfer Policy give the registrant?

It gives you three concrete things: the transfer code, the unlock, and a short list of the only reasons a registrar may say no. ICANN's policy applies to generic domains such as .com, .net and .org. Country-code domains such as .us follow their own registry's rules.

**The transfer code.** Moving a domain to another registrar needs an authorization code, also called an auth code, EPP code or transfer code. ICANN's registrant FAQ is plain: "Your registrar must provide you with the AuthInfo code within five (5) calendar days of your request." Many registrars let you generate it yourself in the account.

**The unlock.** Domains usually carry a transfer lock (shown as `clientTransferProhibited`). The registrar must remove it within five calendar days of the registrant's request if you can't remove it yourself. The policy also says a registrar may not make getting the code or the unlock harder than changing your contact details.

**The reasons a registrar may refuse.** A registrar may deny a transfer only for:

- evidence of fraud
- a reasonable dispute over who the registrant is
- unpaid fees for a *previous* registration period (the domain has to be put on hold first)
- an express objection from the registrant
- the domain is within 60 days of first registration
- the domain is within 60 days of a previous transfer between registrars

The registrar must also say why it refused. It must refuse while there's a pending UDRP case (a trademark dispute), a court order, a transfer dispute, or a 60-day change-of-registrant lock, covered next.

The policy also lists things that are **not** valid reasons, including "nonpayment for a pending or future registration period" and "no response" from the registrant. And if a reseller fell out with its registrar over money, that can't block the transfer when you've paid for the domain.

## Why would the domain be locked for 60 days?

There are two 60-day locks, and the second one catches people out.

1. **After registration or a transfer.** A registrar may refuse to move a domain within 60 days of it being created or transferred to a new registrar. There's no opt-out; you wait.
2. **After a change of registrant.** When the registrant's name, organization or email changes, the registrar must lock the domain against transfers to another registrar for 60 days. The registrar *may* let the registrant opt out before the change, but it doesn't have to.

The second lock shapes your plan. ICANN's advice: "If your ultimate goal is to transfer the domain name, you may want to consider completing the transfer process before changing your contact information." In practice:

- **If you are already the registrant**, get the code and move the domain first, then tidy up the contact details at the new registrar.
- **If the designer is the registrant**, the registrant has to be changed to you first. The registrar asks the current registrant to confirm the change. Ask whether you can opt out of the 60-day lock; if not, the domain stays put for 60 days. The lock only blocks moving to another registrar: the website and email keep working, and the registrant can still change DNS settings.

## How to get your domain back, step by step

Start polite, put everything in writing, and only escalate when a step fails.

1. **Collect your evidence.** Invoices or bank statements showing you paid for the domain, the contract or proposal, emails that refer to "your domain", your business registration documents and a photo ID. A registrar weighing "a reasonable dispute over the identity of the registrant" will want these.
2. **Send a short, specific written request.** Email is fine. Name the domain, ask for one exact thing, and give a reasonable date. For example: "Please send me the transfer code for example.com and remove the transfer lock by Friday the 17th. If the domain is registered in your name, please change the registrant to [your business name] and [your email]." If you owe a final invoice, pay it or say when you will, so money isn't the sticking point.
3. **Go to the registrar yourself.** If you are the registrant, contact the registrar's support directly, explain the account is held by a former vendor, and ask for the code or a change of account. ICANN's [Registrants' Benefits and Responsibilities](https://www.icann.org/resources/pages/benefits-2013-09-16-en) page says you're entitled to clear information on how your registrar handles transfers and disputes. Ask what they need from you, and send the documents from step 1.
4. **Complain to ICANN if the registrar won't follow the rules.** If you are the registrant and the registrar ignores your request for the code or unlock, or refuses for a reason that isn't on the list, file a Transfer complaint through [ICANN Contractual Compliance](https://www.icann.org/compliance/complaint). Be clear about what this can do: ICANN enforces the registrar's contract, and says it "does not have the ability or authority to transfer or return a domain name to anyone." It also has no authority over country-code domains such as .us. For those, contact the registry listed at [IANA](https://www.iana.org/domains/root/db).
5. **Legal options.** If the designer is the registrant and won't change it, this is a contract or property dispute between you and them: a lawyer's demand letter, then court. UDRP only covers trademark abuse. This is not legal advice; ICANN's [lost domain names page](https://www.icann.org/resources/pages/lost-domain-names) lists the routes.

## Should you just register a new domain instead?

Sometimes it's the cheaper choice: if the domain is new, short on links and reviews, and the designer is dragging it out, a new domain and a fresh start can cost less than a fight. Weigh it against what's tied to the old name: your business email, printed materials, your Google Business Profile website link, and years of links pointing at it. If your email runs on that domain, fight for it. If the domain might also be close to expiring, read [what happens when a domain expires](/insights/domain-expired-get-it-back/) now, because an expiry can make all of this moot.
