You own your website only if your business is the **registrant of the domain** and you hold the **top-level login** for each account the site depends on: the registrar, the hosting, the website admin, your email and your Google listing. Paying the bill isn't the same as owning it. Having a login isn't either, if someone else's name is on the account. You can check all of it in about 10 minutes with a free lookup and a few sign-ins.

I do this check for every business I look after, as part of my [website plans for Amarillo and Canyon businesses](/amarillo/). Here's how to do it yourself.

## What does "owning" mean for each piece?

Each account has a level that controls it, and a lower level that only uses it. You want the top level, in your business's name.

| Account | You own it when you are… | A login alone usually means… |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Domain | The **registrant** (ICANN calls it the "Registered Name Holder"), and the registrar account is yours | You can see it, but someone else can move or let it lapse |
| Hosting | The account holder, paying with your card | The host can lock you out if the reseller stops paying |
| Website admin | An administrator, with the ability to export or back up | You can edit pages, not take the site with you |
| Email | An admin of Google Workspace or Microsoft 365 (or similar) | You have a mailbox someone else can delete |
| Google Business Profile | The **primary owner** | A **manager** can edit, but can't add or remove people or remove the profile |
| Search Console | A **verified owner** | Users can view data; owners control who has access |
| Google Analytics | An **Administrator** on the account | Viewers and editors can't manage users |
| Facebook and other social | An admin of the page in your own account | You post; someone else controls it |

The registrant matters most. Under ICANN's [Transfer Policy](https://www.icann.org/resources/pages/transfer-policy-2016-06-01-en), the Registered Name Holder is the party with authority to approve or deny moving the domain, and a registrar must hand them the transfer code within five calendar days of asking. If your designer is the registrant, those rights are theirs, not yours.

## The 10-minute check

Do these in order. Keep a note of what you find; the printable table at the end is built for that.

### 1. Look up your domain (2 minutes)

Go to [ICANN Lookup](https://lookup.icann.org/), ICANN's free tool to "look up the current registration data for domain names," and enter your domain. Note the **registrar**, the **expiration date** and the **name servers**.

The registrant's name is often redacted for privacy, so the lookup may not show *who* owns it. That's what the next step is for.

### 2. Sign in to the registrar (2 minutes)

Log in to the registrar named in the lookup. If you can't, or you don't know which email the account uses, that's your first red flag. Once in, check:

- **Registrant contact:** your business name and an email address you control, not the designer's.
- **Account email:** the one password resets go to. It should be yours.
- **Auto-renew:** on, with a card that won't expire before the renewal date.
- **Expiry date:** matches the lookup. Set your own reminder a month before.

### 3. Check DNS and hosting (1 minute)

The name servers from step 1 tell you who runs your DNS: the settings that send visitors to your website and mail to your inbox. Whoever controls DNS controls both. If the name servers belong to the designer's company or a host you've never heard of, find out who holds that login.

### 4. Sign in to the website admin (1 minute)

Log in to the site's dashboard (WordPress, Wix, Squarespace, Shopify or whatever it's built on). Check that you're an **administrator** and that you can find an export or backup option. If you can't log in at all, you don't control the site.

### 5. Check your email admin (1 minute)

Sign in to the admin console for your business email. If you only have a mailbox and no admin console, ask who the admin is. If the answer is "the web company", your inbox depends on them.

### 6. Check Google Business Profile (1 minute)

Sign in at [business.google.com](https://business.google.com/), open your profile and look at the people with access. Google's [owners and managers](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3403100) page sets out the roles: a profile can have several owners but only one primary owner; owners can add or remove users and delete the profile; managers can do almost everything else. Your business should be the primary owner. Helpers should be managers.

If you can't see the profile at all, someone else controls it. Google's [request ownership](https://support.google.com/business/answer/4566671) process gives the current owner 3 days to respond.

### 7. Check Search Console and Analytics (1 minute)

In [Search Console](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/7687615), go to **Settings → Users and permissions**. Google shows that page only to property owners, so if you can open it, you're an owner; check whether you're listed as verified or delegated. Google notes that a removed owner can re-verify if their verification token is left on the site, so ask whoever set it up to tell you which method they used.

In Google Analytics, go to **Admin** and open **Access management** at the account level, and confirm you have the **Administrator** role. Google won't let the last Administrator remove themselves, so if there's an Administrator, there's someone who can add you.

### 8. Check social pages (1 minute)

Open your Facebook page's settings and check who has full control. Do the same for any other platform where customers find you.

## Red flags in your website contract or setup

None of these are unusual, and most are easy to fix while you and your designer are still on good terms.

- **The designer or agency is listed as the registrant.** It's convenient for them; it's a risk for you. Ask for it to be changed to your business now.
- **The domain is "included" in a monthly package.** Ask whose name it's registered in, and what happens to it if you cancel.
- **You can't export the site.** Some builders and some vendors' own platforms don't let you take the site's design with you. That's a legitimate business model, but you should know before you sign. Your text, photos and domain should still come with you; ask for that in writing.
- **"We'll manage your Google listing" with no access for you.** You should be the primary owner; they can be a manager.
- **No list of what you're paying for.** Each line on the bill should map to an account you can see. If the bill is a single number, see [what a website should cost per month](/insights/website-cost-per-month-what-is-normal/).
- **Logins only in one person's head.** If the designer, or the one employee who knows, vanished tomorrow, could you sign in?

This isn't legal advice. If a contract says the vendor owns the domain or site, a business attorney can tell you what that means for you. ICANN's [registrants' benefits and responsibilities](https://www.icann.org/resources/pages/benefits-2013-09-16-en) is the plain-English starting point on the domain side.

Any "No" is your to-do list. Fix the domain first: it's the one that takes your website and email down with it.

## What to do with the results

- **All yes:** store the logins in a password manager, add a second trusted person, and repeat this check once a year.
- **Some no, designer reachable:** send a short, friendly email listing each account and asking for it to be moved into your name. Most designers will do it. If yours won't hand over the domain, see [your web designer won't give you your domain](/insights/web-designer-wont-give-domain/).
- **Some no, designer gone:** follow the recovery order in [my web designer disappeared](/insights/web-designer-disappeared-get-website-back/): domain, email, website, then Google.
