A small business website costs anywhere from about $20 a month to over $1,000 a month, and both ends can be fair. The running costs (domain, hosting, security certificate) are small: a do-it-yourself builder plan covers them for roughly $17 to $50 a month. Everything above that pays for people: updates, changes, someone answering the phone, and marketing work such as SEO. So $200 a month is normal if you can name what it buys, and expensive if you can't.

The way to judge your bill is to break it into line items. I'll do that below, with published prices for each. For transparency, I sell monthly plans myself: [my Amarillo and Canyon plans](/amarillo/#plans) are $299, $599 and $999, and I list exactly what's in each further down. If you're local and want a second opinion on a bill, the [Amarillo page](/amarillo/) has the details.

## What goes into a monthly website bill?

Up to nine things, and most bills bundle several without listing them. Here's each one, what it is in plain English, and what it costs on its own.

| Line item | What it is | Typical cost on its own |
|---|---|---|
| Domain | Your address, like yourbusiness.com | About $11 a year for a .com at a low-markup registrar (under $1 a month) |
| Hosting | The computer that serves your site | About $17 to $50 a month on a builder plan (Wix, Squarespace), which also includes the next item |
| SSL certificate | The padlock; makes the site "https" | Free from Let's Encrypt, and included by most hosts and builders |
| Email | you@yourbusiness.com | $7 per person per month for Google Workspace Business Starter (annual plan) |
| Maintenance | Software updates, backups, uptime and security monitoring | $89 to $239 a month for published WordPress care plans, hosting not included |
| Changes and support | Someone who edits the site and answers questions | Billed hourly or as hours included in a plan |
| SEO | Work to rank in Google | Most common: $501 to $1,000 a month |
| Google Business Profile management | Keeping your Google listing current: posts, photos, review replies | From about $99 a month per location for automated tools |
| Ads management | Running Google or Facebook ads | A management fee, separate from what you pay the ad platform |

Some notes on the numbers:

- **Domain.** Porkbun lists .com at $11.08 a year, the same for renewal. Cloudflare sells domains at the registry's cost with no markup. If the domain shows up on your bill at $20 or $30 a month, it is not the domain you're paying for.
- **Hosting.** Wix's paid plans run $17.77 to $39.77 a month and Squarespace's $19 to $49 a month for the common tiers, billed annually. Both include hosting and the security certificate, and both include a free domain for the first year on annual plans. A custom-built site on its own hosting can cost more or less, but for a small business site, hosting alone is rarely the expensive part.
- **SSL.** Let's Encrypt, a nonprofit, issues free certificates to more than 700 million websites. A separate "SSL fee" every month is worth a question.
- **Maintenance.** This matters most for WordPress sites, which need plugin and security updates. WP Buffs, a national WordPress care company, charges $89 a month for updates, backups and monitoring, $179 with unlimited small edits and $239 with speed and malware work. It doesn't include hosting. Builder platforms like Wix and Squarespace update their own software, so you need less of this.
- **Changes and support.** In Ahrefs' survey of 439 SEO providers, the most common hourly rate was $75 to $100. That's a reasonable yardstick for web changes too. A plan should say how many hours are included each month.
- **SEO.** In the same Ahrefs survey, the most common monthly SEO retainer was $501 to $1,000 (20.4% of providers), and local SEO averaged $1,557 a month. Backlinko's 2025 survey calls $501 to $1,000 the small business tier. Plenty of small businesses spend less: in WebFX's buyer survey, 43 to 45% of businesses with 1 to 50 employees spend $100 to $500 a month.
- **Google Business Profile.** Merchynt sells an automated management tool at $99 a month per location. A person doing the work (replying to reviews, adding photos, fixing wrong hours) costs more.
- **Ads management.** The fee covers setting up and adjusting the campaigns. The ad spend goes to Google or Meta, and you should see it on their invoice, not buried in a lump sum.

## So is $200 a month normal?

Yes, if it buys hosting, maintenance, a set number of change hours and a person who answers. No, if it buys hosting for a template site you can't log into.

Three common shapes:

| Monthly | What it usually covers |
|---|---|
| $20 to $60 | A builder plan you run yourself, plus email |
| $100 to $350 | Hosting, updates, backups, monitoring and some edit hours, sometimes Google profile upkeep |
| $500 to $1,000+ | All of the above plus local SEO: listings, content, reporting, sometimes ads |

The middle row is where most "website plus support" bills land. The bottom row is mostly marketing, and it should come with reports that show what was done.

## What is normal to bundle?

Domain, hosting, SSL, maintenance and a few change hours are normal to bundle into one price. SEO, ads and Google profile work are services, and each should be listed separately so you can see what you're paying for.

Bundling is fine. What matters is that you can see the parts. A single "website package $349/mo" line is normal on an invoice, but the agreement behind it should say what's inside, what's in your name, and what happens when you leave.

## What are the red flags on a website bill?

The biggest red flag is paying every month for things you can't name or log into. The others follow from it.

1. **You can't name what each dollar buys.** Ask for a written list. A good provider can produce it in a few minutes.
2. **You have no access.** You should have a login to your domain registrar, your website, your Google Business Profile and your analytics. If everything sits in the provider's accounts, you're renting.
3. **The domain isn't registered to you.** Look it up and check whose name is on it. My [10-minute ownership check](/insights/who-owns-your-website-ownership-check/) walks through how.
4. **A long contract with automatic renewal.** A 12-month minimum isn't unusual for a website build, but know the end date, the notice period and how renewal works before you sign.
5. **"SEO" with no reports.** Monthly SEO should come with a monthly account of the work: pages changed, listings fixed, rankings and calls tracked. "We're working on it" isn't a report.
6. **A phone number on your Google listing that isn't yours.** Some marketing packages use tracking numbers. If the number belongs to the vendor, you can lose it when you leave. Google's own guidelines say the phone number on a Business Profile "must be under the direct control of the business."
7. **Fees for free things.** A monthly SSL charge, or a domain billed at many times what it costs.

If several of these apply, read [what you own when you leave a bundled website vendor](/insights/leaving-hibu-thryv-what-you-own/) before you cancel anything.

## How to compare website quotes

Put each quote into the same table, line by line, before you compare totals.

- [ ] Every line item named, with a price or "included"
- [ ] Domain registered in your name, at a registrar where you have the login
- [ ] Your own logins to the website, Google Business Profile and analytics
- [ ] Hours of changes per month, and what happens to unused hours
- [ ] SEO and Google profile work described as tasks, with a monthly report
- [ ] Ad spend billed by Google or Meta directly, separate from the management fee
- [ ] Phone numbers on your listings are yours
- [ ] Contract term, renewal and notice period in writing
- [ ] What you get to keep if you leave: content, images, domain, the site itself

1. Pull your last three website invoices and write each line item next to the table above.
2. Look up who your domain is registered to.
3. Log into your Google Business Profile and check that you are the primary owner and the phone number is yours.
4. Ask your provider for a written list of what's included and when the contract ends.
5. If anything doesn't match, decide whether to renegotiate or move, and do it before the next renewal date.
